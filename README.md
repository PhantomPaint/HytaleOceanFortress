# Ocean Pirate Fortress

I wanted to give the ocean something worth finding: a fortress that stands out from a distance, feels like a real pirate stronghold up close, and rewards players for exploring it. The goal is a compact dungeon with a clear destination, different ways to reach it, and enough optional spaces that it doesn't feel like one long corridor.

## The Original Idea

The main inspiration was the Forsaken Fortress from *The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker*. I liked the idea of spotting a big, slightly intimidating structure in the middle of the ocean and wondering what is inside. The searchlights, vertical towers, and tight passages make that place feel guarded even before you fight anything.

I also looked at real fortresses built directly on the water. They often have a strong silhouette, thick outer walls, and buildings added because people actually needed them. I wanted to borrow that logic rather than just build a castle that happens to be sitting in the sea. A pirate outpost, prison, and base of operations gave me a reason to put different types of rooms into the same structure.

The fortress should be one main mass, towers that break up the shape, and a clear highest point. Up close, the mood changes. The exterior should look defensive and weathered, while the interior feels more cramped, warm, and used. Damage from earlier attacks, especially cannon holes, can help tell that story and create interesting openings in the layout.

## Four Design Pillars

These are the rules I kept coming back to while deciding what belongs in the dungeon.

### 1. Guided, Not Linear

The captain's tower is the overall destination, but I don't want players to follow one obvious hallway to get there. Side routes are fine as long as they lead to something interesting, reconnect to a known space, or offer a different approach.

### 2. Respect the Sandbox

Hytale is a sandbox, so I don't want the dungeon to pretend the player cannot build, climb, or mine. The main route needs to work for someone who just wants to explore normally. At the same time, finding an opening in a wall or making your own shortcut should feel like a valid decision. The trade-off is that players may skip parts of the level. I would rather design around that possibility than lock down every creative solution.

### 3. A Believable Pirate Stronghold

Every major room needs a job. A kitchen should connect naturally to the dining area; storage needs a sensible way in and out; a prison should look like a space the pirates can actually guard. I don't need perfect real-world architecture, but I want players to understand the place by looking at it. Props, materials, damage, and the relationship between rooms should tell the story without needing written lore in every corner.

### 4. Curiosity Should Pay Off

Optional spaces should reward players for noticing things. That reward does not always have to be a chest. A hidden staircase, a safer route, or a useful vantage point can be just as satisfying. I want secrets to be hinted at through the environment: an odd gap, a broken section of wall, light coming from somewhere unexpected, or jumping from beam to beam. It should feel like the player figured something out, not like they randomly mined the correct block.

**Design check:** Whenever I add a room, shortcut, or piece of decoration, I can ask: Does it help navigation, support the pirate theme, reward curiosity, or improve an encounter? If it does none of these, I probably need a better reason for it.

## Layout and Player Flow


### The Main Route and the Detours

I want the first approach to establish a simple goal: get into the fortress and work your way up. Inside, the level can be much less direct. Narrow hallways and staircases should make it feel like a defensive building, but I would avoid stacking too many identical intersections. Players should be able to remember major spaces and understand when they have made progress.

A side path needs a purpose. It might avoid a guarded room, lead through the prison, reveal a treasure space, or reconnect at a higher point. The idea is to have choices that change how players experience the fortress, not just several doors that lead to the same corridor.

## A Place That Feels Used

### Room Functions Before Decoration

The pirates need somewhere to eat, sleep, store supplies, keep weapons, and hold prisoners. That gives the level a natural list of spaces: kitchen, dining hall, storage, armory, living quarters, prison, and finally the captain's arena above. I like using these functions as a starting point because they help decide both the room layout and the props. A storage room can feel crowded and practical; a prison should feel controlled and uncomfortable; the captain's space can be more distinct and easier to remember.

Some repeated rooms are completely fine. Not every bedroom needs its own gimmick. The important part is that the big spaces have a clear identity and players can tell where they are. That also makes the building easier to navigate. A recognizable dining hall is much more useful as a landmark than another generic stone corridor.

### Damage as More Than Decoration

The fortress should show signs of earlier fights, especially around the outside walls. Cannon damage is a good opportunity because it can do two things at once: make the place look old and give the player a possible route or view into another area. I would rather use a few meaningful damage spots than scatter random holes everywhere. That way the damage feels intentional and can become part of the level design.

## Player Choice and Sandbox Freedom

### Fighting Through vs. Finding Another Way

The straightforward route should remain a valid option. A player can push through occupied spaces and take the fights as they come. Other routes should offer the possibility of avoiding some of that pressure, reaching a different position, or finding extra resources first. I initially thought of this as "stealth or guns blazing," but that only works as a proper stealth route if enemies can actually detect and lose track of the player in a predictable way. If a stealth mechanic was in the game, I would add more dead spots where the player can hide.

### What if Players Break the Level?

A player may climb the outside walls, mine through a room, or build straight toward the tower. I think that is part of what makes a sandbox dungeon interesting. The important design question is not "How do I stop them?" but "What happens if they do?" An unexpected shortcut can be a cool discovery.

For that reason, I would check the most obvious climb and mining routes. The main path should not rely on the player following invisible rules, and any reward that requires finding a secret wall should have at least a small environmental hint.

**Important trade-off:** I want to leave room for creative solutions, but still protect the pacing and readability of the main experience. That balance needs actual in-game testing, not just a rule written on paper.

If a player just wants to clear the boss to farm materials, there should obviously be a fast and easy way. A player can also use blocks to box themselves in to have some security.

## Encounters and the Pirate Captain

### Encounters Along the Way

Tight passages can create pressure, but putting several enemies in every narrow hallway would get frustrating quickly. I want encounters to vary between guarded rooms, transitions where players can take a breath, and areas that reward checking the surroundings before running in. Boom Barrels should be placed at strategic positions to signal to the player that they may be of importance.

### Boss Concept

The captain belongs at the highest tower, looking out over the water with a spyglass and planning the next raid. I don't think this dungeon needs a boss with a huge list of phases. My preferred concept is a mostly melee-focused captain who can occasionally order a cannon barrage. The targeted area would be clearly marked, then hit after a short delay. This gives the player a readable reason to move, and the mechanic fits the pirate theme.

Two or three additional pirates could create pressure.

### Ideas I Considered, but Would Not Prioritize

I also thought about a burning sword attack, a healing fruit ability, and a grappling-hook move. The fruit could drag the fight out without making it better; the hook does not naturally fit the arena I have in mind; and the fire attack may be one mechanic too many.

## Procedural Generation

I chose to hand-build the fortress instead of using prefabs and picking from there. That gives me direct control over the shape of the building, vertical connections, sightlines, and encounter spaces. I chose it specifically for this version, but have also been experimenting with the idea of using multiple layouts and rooms that get chosen at random. Ultimately, I decided against it just to present one version of the fortress.

## New Resource Ideas

Below are some new resources that could be unique to the Ocean or Pirate Faction. Since loot is getting a rework anyway, I decided to put my own thoughts and ideas here.

- **Gunpowder** — Can be used to create new explosive items.
- **Manana (Mana Banana)** — Boosts mana/mana regeneration; can be used as an ingredient.
- **Painapple** — Boosts strength/critical hits; can be used as an ingredient.
- **Coins** — Some sort of currency.
- **Cannon-Rune** — Allows the player to call down their own cannonballs.
